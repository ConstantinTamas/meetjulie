Method

Why it holds up

A systematic review has to be repeatable by someone who was not there.

The published method tells you what a review must do and what it must report. It does not tie that report back to the work: the methods section is written afterwards, by the same people, from notes and spreadsheets. Julie's record is produced by the run itself, while the work is done: the standards applied as published, the additions declared as additions, and your decisions recorded under the name you gave.

EXAMPLE · A GUIDELINE UPDATEThe F2F consensus meeting is not the last reading this work gets. After the panel there is a journal, and after the journal a methods editor who was not in the room.

  • Checked against PRISMA 2020, item by item

    The chain has been compared with the official checklist and flow diagram, item by item. What you get from a run is a methods report built from that run's own record, and where it cannot produce something the checklist asks for, it names the item and hands it back to you rather than reading as complete.

  • CEBM and GRADE as published, not a simplified reading of them

    Ratings follow Oxford CEBM (March 2009) or classic GRADE, chosen per project. Every rating includes its source support and methodological rationale. Any project-specific additions are identified separately from the published standard.

  • Decisions are recorded under a name

    Julie asks who is deciding before she writes anything down, and that is the name the record carries and the methods report reads from. Her own name never goes into those fields, because a machine name there would misstate how the review was made.

  • It stops rather than guesses

    Missing or inconsistent information is brought to your attention before the work continues. You can see what needs resolving and which decisions remain yours.

  • Keeps the review current and traceable

    Your results remain connected to the material and decisions behind them. When that material changes, Julie shows you which results need review.

  • AI use, in a form you can publish

    Every paid stage leaves the record your disclosure statement is built from, in the vocabulary the field's own position statement asks for. You do not have to reconstruct afterwards which model did what at which stage: it is written down while the work is being done.

What it costs, and who decides

free

Setting the review up, checking what is missing, seeing where it stands, and estimating what a stage would cost. None of it spends anything.

paid

The stages that read and assess the literature: screening, the eligibility check, extraction, rating, linking the evidence to the recommendations, the brief and the drafting.

the gate

Every paid stage is estimated first, runs only under a ceiling you set and an approval in your name, and reports afterwards what it actually spent.

Julie is in pilot use at the moment, so there is no licence fee: you pay the processing costs directly, and nothing on top. What a stage comes to depends on how many records you screen and how many full texts you process, which is why none of them starts without your approval.

What Julie does not do

  • You approve and run the searches. Julie helps draft strategies for PubMed, Embase and CENTRAL from your questions. You settle the final strategies and run them in the databases. The full strategies and search details still belong in the manuscript.
  • She does not make the decisions that are yours. No inclusion call, no proposed meeting outcome, no rating in your place.
  • She does not quietly substitute one instrument for another. The instrument follows the study design. Where a question could not be answered the way the instrument intends, the answer is named and flagged rather than quietly recoded into a milder one.
  • She does not approve spending. The ceiling and the name are yours.
  • She does not write clinical sense. Whether a sentence is clinically right, or accurate but misleading, is something you read.
  • She does not replace expert reading. The checks establish that the material is internally consistent and traceable. The scientific judgement stays with you.

What does the benchmark actually measure?

A headline accuracy score needs context. Elicit’s May 2026 extraction benchmark reported 95.6% correct on methods, participants and interventions, with outcome extraction excluded. SciSpace’s 200-query benchmark measured search relevance using AI judges; it did not establish complete study retrieval or GRADE accuracy.

These are useful measurements of specific tasks. For a systematic review, the questions go further: were eligible studies retained, were critical results extracted correctly, and were certainty judgements justified?
